Renters’ Rights Act

Headline points on the Renters’ Rights Act and what you need to know: who it applies to, what has changed about your contract, your rent and your right to stay, and what has stayed the same.

Who does this apply to?

This legislation change applies to Private Rented Accommodation in England.

The changes do not affect the rights of:

  • Those who live in privately run Halls of residence (off campus)
  • University Halls of Residence (on campus)
  • Lodgers who live with their landlord
  • Social housing tenants

What is changing: your contract

From May 1, 2026, the Renters’ Rights Act will transform private renting in England by abolishing fixed-term tenancies. Assured Shorthold Tenancies (AST) are out… and Assured Periodic Tenancies (APT) are in!

For most tenants, this means if they have a fixed-term agreement they will automatically move to a rolling contract.

A rolling contract (also known as a periodic contract (APT) is an agreement that continues to renew automatically at the end of each specified period—usually monthly, without a fixed end date. It stays in effect until one party gives notice to terminate.

Tenants won't need a break clause (break clause: some old housing contracts (AST’S) may have contained a clause to allow the tenants or landlords to leave the contract earlier than the fixed terms dates laid out in the contract) As tenants will no longer be locked into a fixed contract - between specific dates.

What is an AST?

An AST usually has a fixed term ie: 6 or 12 months. An APT your tenancy no longer has a fixed-term date.

Key features of an Assured Periodic Tenancy (APT)
  • Rolling Basis: It continues indefinitely, usually on a monthly until either the tenant or landlord ends it.
  • Flexibility: It offers more flexibility than a fixed-term contract, allowing tenants to leave with less long-term commitment.
What happens to my current contract on the 1st of May?

If you already signed your tenancy before 1st May, don’t worry, you don’t need sign anything new straight away.

From 1st May 2026, your tenancy will automatically switch from an AST to an Assured Periodic Tenancy (APT)

Your landlord is required to send you an official government factsheet explaining what’s changed. You should receive this by 31st May 2026 at the latest.

You might also get a separate notice called “Ground 4A”, this relates to specific situations where landlords can regain possession of a property. More on this below.

Ending your tenancy

If you’re thinking about moving out, you’ll need to give your landlord (or letting agent) 2 months’ written notice. The notice must be in writing (email usually counts, but check your agreement)

If you’re on a joint tenancy, things work a bit differently now:

Any one tenant can give notice to end the tenancy. But… that notice applies to everyone on the contract

That means if one person decides to leave and gives notice, the whole household will need to move out at the end of the 2-month notice period, unless you can agree something different with your landlord.

So before making any moves, it’s definitely worth having a chat with your housemates first!

For the practical steps – notice periods, finding a replacement tenant, and getting your deposit back – see Leaving your tenancy.

Rent in advance and rent increases

Rent in advance

Been asked to pay loads of rent upfront? That’s changed

In the past, if you didn’t meet certain criteria (like having a UK guarantor), landlords or agents might have asked for 6 or even 12 months’ rent upfront.

Good news — they can’t do that anymore. Under the new rules, landlords and agents can only ask for:

  • 1 month’s rent in advance, and a deposit. That’s it. No larger upfront payments just to secure a place.

Rent increases

Rents can only be increased once per year, and landlords can only increase them once they have provided you with the right paperwork – known as a Section 13 notice.

What is a Section 13 notice?

For an Assured Periodic Tenancy (remember these at the start of this guide), landlords can increase the rent once per year. Landlords must give tenants at least one month’s notice of the change in writing if the rent is paid monthly.

Pets, benefits and children

Requesting to keep a pet

You now have the right to ask your landlord if you can keep a pet in your home.

Landlords can’t just say no without a good reason — they’re not allowed to “unreasonably refuse” your request.

So what counts as a reasonable reason? A landlord might say no if, for example:

  • The property is too small for the type of pet
  • The building has rules (e.g. no pets allowed in flats)
  • Keeping a pet could cause damage or safety issues

What this means for you: you’ve got a stronger position than before, but it’s not an automatic yes. If your landlord does refuse, they should be able to clearly explain why.

Welfare benefits and tenants with children

A landlord cannot refuse a tenancy because someone has children or receives benefits.

Evictions and Ground 4A

Bye bye Section 21s – no fault evictions

If you had an Assured Shorthold Tenancy (AST) landlords could evict tenants by serving a Section 21 notice – these were known as ‘no-fault evictions’, the landlord didn’t need a reason to issue them. With the Renters Rights Act, under an Assured Period Tenancy (APT) your landlord can no longer evict you without a specific reason.

There are strict criteria under which your landlord can evict you – such as non-payment of rent. Landlords will need to apply to court to get an eviction notice on any of the grounds allowed and provide evidence that convinces the court that the grounds have been satisfied.

Student special: Ground 4A

If you are living in a rented property that is classed as a House of Multiple Occupancy (HMO) – that is, a property rented to different tenants from more than one household who are sharing, e.g., bathrooms, kitchens, etc. – like a group of students living together as friends. Then there are specific rules which relate to ending student tenancies.

Find out about what is classed as a HMO under Reading Borough Council here: Houses in multiple occupation (Reading Borough Council)

Your tenancy agreement will still be periodic, however for student landlord’s whose tenants live in a HMO - they can serve a notice based on Ground 4A.

This allows the landlord to gain the possession of the property in line with the academic year (i.e. handing back the property to the landlord between 1st June to 30th September).

In simple terms

Even though your contract is rolling, your landlord can still plan around the student cycle and ask for the property back over the summer period using this rule.

More information on the 4A notice can be found here: Eviction notice at the end of a student tenancy (Shelter)

So what is staying the same?

Guarantors

You can still be asked to provide a Guarantor, or use a company Guarantor, as a student tenant.

Reading Students’ Union has a partnership with Housing Hand to offer you preferential rates to use the service. More information can be found here: Housing Hand – Reading Students’ Union rates

Deposits

You will still be asked to pay a deposit, which should be held in a government-backed deposit scheme. The amount of the deposit can be up to 5 weeks' rental value.

All deposits must still be paid into a government-backed deposit scheme. More information on the schemes can be found here: Deposit protection schemes and landlords (GOV.UK)

Holding fee

A holding deposit is money you pay to a landlord or letting agent to reserve a property before you sign a tenancy agreement. Landlords and agents cannot take more than the cost of 1 week’s rent for the property as a holding deposit.

Tenants Fee Act 2019

This legislation supports renters in not being charged for services from Agents and Landlords. More information can be found here: Renters’ Rights Act: changes for private renters (Shelter)
